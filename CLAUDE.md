# Puzzle app

A picture-puzzle web app for iPad (plain HTML/CSS/JS, no build step), hosted on GitHub Pages from `main` at https://hacudr.github.io/puzzle/.

The home page shows three collections, in this order: Impressionism to Modern, Romantics & Realism, and Old Masters & Ancient Art (`puzzles/collections.json`, each with a `cover`). Their cards are wide, the picture on the left and the name on the right, so a collection may name a `focus` as an artist does (below); Degas's Four Dancers takes `"50% 25%"` to keep the dancers' heads in view. Their cards carry no italic line and no count of puzzles, only "Collection 1:" and so on above the name, in the same large letters. Tapping one opens a page of its artists, with no categories between, and tapping an artist opens a page of their puzzles. Each puzzle in `puzzles/puzzles.json` names its `artist`, and `puzzles/artists.json` lists the artists, each with a `note` (the italic line, such as "French, 1840–1926"), a `period` and a `cover` (one of their puzzle images). The artist cards crop the cover to an upright 4:5 shape, with the label over its lower part, so a cover whose people would lose their heads to the crop names the part to keep in view in `focus`, as in `"focus": "50% 0%"` for the top (CSS `object-position`); look at a new cover on its card and add one if a head is cut off. `puzzles/periods.json` lists the periods, each with the `collection` it belongs to; a collection's artists appear in the order of their periods there, and within a period in the order of `artists.json`, except that the artist a collection names as its `start` comes first: Claude Monet in Impressionism to Modern, where the user begins. Old Masters & Ancient Art runs from newest to oldest: Baroque, Renaissance, Anglo-Saxon and Saxon Art, Ancient Art, and within each the latest-born artist (or latest culture) first, by the order of `periods.json` and `artists.json`. A collection marked `"order": "surname"` lists its artists by surname instead (by `sort` where the last word of the name is wrong); Romantics & Realism is. Female Artists is still in `periods.json` and a woman artist still carries `"also": ["Female Artists"]`, but the app no longer shows categories, so neither has any effect for now. The home page shows only the collections: the list of every artist by surname and the Surprise Me buttons, on the home page and the solved screen, were taken out at the user's wish. An artist's `sort` word in `artists.json`, left from that list, now only orders a collection marked `"order": "surname"`. An artist with no `period` lands in a collection of its own, Other pictures. An artist's `bio` in `artists.json` (one or two sentences on the artist and the movement they belonged to, written for the app rather than copied, so it needs no credit) shows under their name at the top of their page, justified to the width of the cards below, with their italic `note` at its end and a fine rule under it. Every artist and culture has one, and a new artist needs one too.

Each collection is to be sold on its own at $4.99, and for now the buying is only simulated: no payment is wired up. Until a collection is bought, its card and its artists' cards are toned sepia (a brown laid over the picture in the `color` blend, `.tone` in `style.css`, not a CSS filter) with a small padlock. The first collection is the way in: its card and its first artist's (Monet's) stay in colour, and that artist's first 10 pictures can be played (`FREE`, `playable` in `app.js`). A sepia collection can still be opened to see its artists; tapping a sepia artist, a locked puzzle, or Next Puzzle into one opens the pay screen (`#paywall`, `openPaywall`), where any one, two or all three collections are chosen and "bought". Buying keeps the names in `puzzle.bought` in localStorage and lifts the sepia off slowly. Settings has Unlock collections and Reset purchases, for trying it again.

While the app is hosted in the open, a password page (`#lock` in `index.html`) covers everything, the splash too, until the code is given; the page holds only its SHA-256 hash, and each device is asked once (`puzzle.unlocked` in localStorage). It only keeps out the casual visitor: the pictures and lists can still be fetched by their addresses.

Each time the app is opened afresh it starts on a splash: one picture filling the screen under a title card, "Welcome to Art History · Best played with music", and "Tap to begin", put away with a tap. With the music on, that tap starts `music/welcome.mp3`, the opening of Debussy's Clair de lune (a CC BY recording, so the splash names its player under the picture's title), which plays softly over the gallery for thirty seconds and then dies away over five; it dies away sooner, over a second and a half, when a puzzle is opened. It waits for the tap because a browser allows no sound before the first touch. The picture is never one with a `content` tag, whatever the settings show, and each opening takes one not shown before until every such picture has had a turn. A reload in the same tab does not bring the splash back.

Every button answers a tap with the stroke of a paintbrush on canvas, under half a second of soft noise with no pitch to it (`sound.tap()`, made by the app itself and so no file with rights to clear). Settings has two sound controls. Sound effects, a switch, covers this tap sound, the notes the pieces play as they fall into place, and the swords, hooves and knocking at the end. Music is a slider of three stops, Off, On: Short and On: Long (`setMusic` in `app.js`), with a line saying what it does; it covers the splash's music and the music that ends a solved puzzle. Long (`puzzle.long`, kept apart from `puzzle.music` in `sound.js`) makes that ending thirty seconds of the recording in place of a few: `music/long/<name>.mp3`, cut from the same full recording at the very place the short clip starts, at its level unless that would clip, and dying away over the last four seconds. A tune marked `short` in `CLIPS` in `sound.js` has no long cut (the Kyrie, whose full recording is on Musopen behind a sign-in), and one whose long file cannot be had plays its short one. The full recordings are kept in `music/sources/`, which is git-ignored. The music slider's stops and Off for Kids mode stay silent. On an iPhone the audio can die while the app is put away or the screen locked, and no setting is to blame: every touch calls `sound.unlock()`, which replaces an audio context that has run and since been away, or whose clock has stopped, with a new one (and fetches again what the open puzzle ends on). It has not been tried on a phone, only reasoned from how iOS behaves; if sound is lost again, that is where to look.

A puzzle that has been solved opens from its card on the finished picture in its frame, not on the pieces, and Solve Again deals it afresh; one begun again and left half done opens where it was left. On Expert that takes having solved it in black and white. The bar at the top of the gallery's pages, with the way back and the settings, stays put while the page scrolls under it (`.top`). Every card crops its picture to the same upright shape, so each puzzle's card carries a small outline in its top corner of the picture's own shape, tall, wide or square (`markShape` in `app.js`, `.shape` in `style.css`), drawn once the thumbnail is in.

Every artist is a chapter, opened one picture at a time in their order (`stateOf` in `app.js`); every artist's card can be opened at any time. Next Puzzle on the solved screen goes on to the artist's next unsolved picture, and once all of theirs are solved, to the next artist's first unsolved one, in the order the collections show them, the last artist of a collection leading on to the first of the next (`nextPuzzle` in `app.js`). The first picture not yet solved is Up next, shown only as a blur of its colours from `puzzles/veils/`, which is brought down to 8 pixels across so that only its colours and broad shapes are left and the picture cannot be told; those after it show the same blur, with a question mark for a title and the year as the italic line, and none of them loads the picture itself. Solving one lifts the blur off it and turns the next card round to ring it when the artist's page is shown again. Solving an artist's last picture brings up a congratulations card over the framed picture, Chapter complete with their name, under a gilt laurel that draws itself while flakes of gold leaf drift down; the last picture of a whole collection brings Collection complete instead, with more leaf (`showCheer` in `app.js`, `.cheer` in `style.css`). Continue, or a tap beside the card, puts it away. The collection and artist cards keep their covers; the splash may show any picture, hidden or not. Run `node veil.js all` (or a category's name) after adding pictures, to make their blurs; it skips those already made. Making the blur is part of adding a puzzle, as the thumbnail is.

On a solved puzzle, tapping the painting, or the ⓘ after its title, opens a wall card. Its title stays put and the text between it and Close scrolls, fading at an edge with more beyond and showing a small arrow that reads on until the end is in view. For a picture with a story in `puzzles/stories.json` (about 340: the scripture, saint's life, myth, literature or history behind it, what this picture shows, and passages copied word for word from public-domain editions, each named in its `sources` and credited at the foot of the card), the card tells that story; it is built by `node stories/build.js` from `stories/spec/`, whose downloaded texts sit in the git-ignored `stories/texts/`, and `mockups/stories-review.html` shows it all for review. Where a story has no passage to quote, and for every picture without a story, the card gives a few sentences on the picture from `puzzles/blurbs.json`: the opening of its English Wikipedia article where it has one (found through the `wikidata` id in its record, CC BY-SA 4.0, so the card names and links the article and the licence), and otherwise the museum's own description: Cleveland's (CC0), the Art Institute of Chicago's (its API gives `description` under CC BY 4.0, so the card names and links that licence too) or the National Gallery of Art's from its open data (CC0). The Met gives none. Where a picture's record leads to no article, its entry in `puzzles.json` may name one in `article`: the painting's own (Hudson River School, Ginevra de' Benci), or else one on its sitter, place or kind of object (Misia Sert, Kynance Cove, Lamassu, Anglo-Saxon brooches); `describe.js` drops the opening brackets of pronunciation and other scripts from every article. A picture with none of these has lines of verse, or a passage of prose, from `puzzles/verses.json` (139 of them: Shakespeare, Keats, Wordsworth, Tennyson, Teasdale, Whitman, Homer, Beowulf and so on, chosen to fit the picture), each cut word for word from a public-domain edition at Project Gutenberg, which the card names and links; `node verses/build.js` builds it from `verses/spec.js`, downloading the editions into the git-ignored `verses/texts/`. A new picture with no story or description wants a passage added there. A picture with none of the three has no ⓘ, and tapping it does nothing. Kids mode hides it, since an article can speak plainly of what the settings hide. `node describe.js all` (or a category's name) writes the text; `--records <folder>` also reads source records kept elsewhere. WikiArt is not used: its terms forbid commercial use. Run it again after adding pictures.

A solved painting can be looked at closely: the magnifier after its title, or two fingers spread on the framed picture, open it alone on a dark ground (`#zoom`, `openZoom` in `app.js`), where it is pinched or double-tapped larger, up to six times the size that fits, wheeled with a mouse, and dragged about; pinched back well below that size, or with its ×, it closes. It shows the puzzle copy, so 2048 px is as much detail as there is. A single tap on the painting still opens the wall card.

A solved puzzle hangs in a carved, gilt frame, a photograph cut out of its background: `frames/gilt-frame.webp`, and the same frame turned on its side, `frames/gilt-frame-landscape.webp`, for a picture wider than it is tall. It carries a fine dark-brown outline round the carving, about 2.5 pixels of the file, drawn into it, with 8 clear pixels on each side to hold it. The frame is drawn in nine pieces, so its corners keep their shape and only the runs between them stretch to the picture; the widths of its sides are set on `.plate` in `style.css`. Settings has a Frame choice between this one, Ornate, and Minimalist, the plain gilt edge the styles draw by themselves (`.frame`, put on by `.plate.plain`); the choice is remembered, and Ornate is the usual one. The photograph is the user's own, as `frames/CREDITS.md` records; a frame from anywhere else needs its photographer's CC0, a frame not being a flat work.

## Rights: everything must be fit for a paid app

The app is to be sold to users in the United States. Assume that of every file that ships with it: pictures, music, sound effects, fonts and icons. Nothing goes in unless it may be used commercially, and whatever a licence asks for by way of credit is given inside the app, not only in the repository. When the rights to something cannot be shown, leave it out and say so.

**Artwork** must be in the public domain in the United States. That means the artist died more than 70 years ago and the work was first published more than 95 years ago (before 1931, as of 2026); for an artist who died in the last hundred years, check both. A museum that releases a work as open access or CC0 has made that check already.

**The file** of a picture has rights of its own, apart from the painting's. Take only:

- a file a museum releases as CC0 or open access (the National Gallery of Art, the Met, Cleveland, the Art Institute of Chicago, the Rijksmuseum), or
- a file on Wikimedia Commons marked "Public domain" or CC0, being a plain, square-on reproduction of a flat work.

Never take a file under a licence with NC or ND in it. Do not take CC BY or CC BY-SA pictures either: the app has nowhere to credit a photographer. A photograph that shows the frame, the wall or the room, or a work that is not flat (a sculpture, a painted shield), is the photographer's own work and needs their CC0, whatever the age of the thing shown; a thin edge of frame or stretcher on a CC0 file may be cropped off in the puzzle copy. Do not download from a museum's own site when its terms forbid commercial use; they bind as a contract even where no copyright exists.

**Record the source.** Every picture has an entry in a file beside the originals, `puzzles/images/<surname>.<source>.json` (`nga`, `met`, `aic` for the Art Institute of Chicago, `cma` for Cleveland, `commons`, or `user` for a photograph the user took themselves, square-on and without the frame), giving the page it came from and its licence. A picture with no such record has not been cleared.

**Italy.** Italian law asks for permission and a fee for commercial reproductions of works in Italian state collections, public domain or not. It has no force in the United States, which is one reason the app is sold there only; the Commons record marks such pictures with `italianHeritageNotice`. Raise it with the user before the app is offered in Italy or the rest of Europe.

**Music and sounds.** The composition must be in the public domain, and the recording public domain, CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA, never NC or ND. Each recording is listed in `music/CREDITS.md` with its player, licence and source, and one under CC BY or CC BY-SA carries its `licence` in `CLIPS` in `sound.js`, so that the solved screen names who is playing. A sound effect needs the same record of where it came from, kept in `sound-effects/CREDITS.md`; the three there now are the user's own recordings.

**Fonts** must allow being bundled in a paid app (the SIL Open Font Licence does), with the licence text kept beside them.

Not yet cleared, to be settled before the app is sold:

- The pictures by Michalina Janoszanka (died 1952, so the 95-year test matters), Virginia Frances Sterrett, the Hudson River School and all but one by Artemisia Gentileschi have no record of where their files came from.
- Joseph Stella died in 1946, so the 95-year test matters. That each picture was published before 1931 rests on its Commons tag alone, and the Met, Chicago and Cleveland mark their own Stellas as not in the public domain. Two are weaker still: Purissima, which Commons tags as unpublished, and Coney Island, which the Met gave to Commons as CC0 but no longer lists as open access.
- `music/avemaria.mp3` is marked Public Domain at the Free Music Archive and CC0 at Commons, but the file there carries an older tag reading CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, and Commons has not reviewed the licence. The page's word is the later one; it wants confirming.
- The solved screen names the player of a licensed recording but not the licence, which CC BY and CC BY-SA ask to be named and linked. The app needs a credits page for that.
- `fonts/` has no copy of the Open Font Licence that Spectral and Averia Serif come under.
- The About cards' text from the Art Institute of Chicago (8 of them) comes under CC BY 4.0, as its API says, but also under the terms of artic.edu, which have not been read. The card names the museum and links the licence; confirm the terms allow use in a paid app, or drop those eight back to no card.
- The source records of the Met puzzles (Ancient Art, Anglo-Saxon and Saxon Art) are not in this folder's `puzzles/images/`, only in the `puzzle - monetize` folder beside it, which `describe.js` was pointed at with `--records`. Since `puzzles/images/` is git-ignored, keep a copy of them here, or somewhere backed up.
- Paton's Study from Nature: Inveruglas comes from the Yale Center for British Art's own image server, not Commons, since the Commons copy is only 1920 px. Yale releases images of public-domain works for any use, but its photograph shows the frame and mount; the puzzle is cropped to the painted sheet. Confirm Yale's open-access terms cover it, or fall back to the smaller Commons file (CC0).
- Caravaggio's Saint Francis in Prayer and Saint Francis in Meditation carry each other's names on Wikipedia, which calls the Cremona picture Meditation and the Rome one Prayer. Each About card is matched to its picture by Wikidata, so its text is right, but it opens under the other title. Scholars use both names for both; decide whether to rename the puzzles.

## When the user adds images

The user drops original pictures into `puzzles/images/` and says little more than "I added some images". Whenever there are pictures in `puzzles/images/` that are not yet in `puzzles/puzzles.json`, do all of the following for each one without being asked and without asking first:

1. **Puzzle copy** — write a web-sized copy to `puzzles/<slug>.webp` (lowercase, hyphenated name, starting with the artist's surname, as in `monet-the-japanese-footbridge.webp`), longest side at most 2048 px. That is as large as an iPad can show; anything bigger only costs download time. WebP at quality 75 is a little over half the size of the JPEG and looks the same. Never modify or delete the originals in `puzzles/images/`.

   ```bash
   ffmpeg -y -v error -i "puzzles/images/<original>" -vf "scale='if(gt(iw,ih),min(2048,iw),-2)':'if(gt(iw,ih),-2,min(2048,ih))':flags=lanczos" -c:v libwebp -quality 75 -compression_level 6 "puzzles/<slug>.webp"
   ```

2. **Thumbnail** — write a copy whose shorter side is 600 px to `puzzles/thumbs/<slug>.webp`, using exactly the same file name as the puzzle copy. (The cards are upright and crop to fill, so a wide picture needs its height, not its width, to be 600.) The collection cards load this; a missing thumbnail falls back to the full picture, which is slow on the iPad.

   ```bash
   ffmpeg -y -v error -i "puzzles/<slug>.webp" -vf "scale='if(gt(iw,ih),-2,600)':'if(gt(iw,ih),600,-2)':flags=lanczos" -c:v libwebp -quality 75 -compression_level 6 "puzzles/thumbs/<slug>.webp"
   ```

   Before either copy is made, be sure the picture passes the rights section above, and that its source and licence are on record. One that does not pass gets no puzzle; tell the user which and why.

3. **Puzzle entry** — look at the picture, then add an entry to `puzzles/puzzles.json` with the same fields the existing entries use (`image`, `artist`, `name`, `scientific` for the italic line, plus any optional fields the neighbouring entries carry, such as `music`: `moonlight`, `clair`, `pathetique`, `arabesque`, `flaxen`, `reflets`, `lente`, `gondola1`, `gondola2`, `gondola3`, `gnossienne`, `gnossienne2` (the same piece's second theme), `spring` (Mendelssohn's Spring Song), `wings` (On Wings of Song), `largo` (Handel's Ombra mai fu), `blacksmith` (The Harmonious Blacksmith) or `hornpipe` (the Water Music hornpipe); a puzzle without one takes the next tune in that order by its position in the list, unless its `content` has `violence` or `frightening`, when it takes the next of the stormy tunes, which are played on the lute where the others are played on the piano: `summer`, `diesirae`, `strife`, `lamentoso`, `mountainking`, `baldmountain` or `newworld` (Wagner's Ride of the Valkyries was taken out at the owner's wish and is not to come back); every tune ends on a few seconds of a recording kept in `music/`, whose sources and licences are in `music/CREDITS.md`). Give the entry `"sacred": true` if its subject is religious: a scene from the Bible, a saint, a Madonna, a prophet. Such a picture takes the next of the sacred tunes in place of the piano ones (`kyrie`, `avemaria` and `cantique`, Fauré's Cantique de Jean Racine, in turn; any tune in `sound.js` marked `sacred` joins them), unless its `content` makes it stormy, when it keeps the stormy tunes. A church seen as a building, a churchman's portrait and a figure from myth are not religious pictures. Give the entry `"effect": "sword"` only if the picture is one of violence or menace in which a sword has a part, a battle, a killing or a threat with a sword in view, so that a clash of swords from `sound-effects/Sword.m4a` sounds with the ending music. Such a picture carries the `violence` tag below. A sword that is only worn or held, as in a portrait, a knight at rest or a saint with the sword that is their sign, does not count, nor does a dagger, a lance or a commander's baton, nor a violent picture in which no sword can be seen. Give it `"effect": "gallop"` if the picture shows a horse, ridden, harnessed or loose, so that hooves from `sound-effects/Gallop.m4a` sound there instead; a horse too small to pick out does not count. Give it `"effect": "knock"` if the picture shows Christ knocking at a door or standing by one, as in The Light of the World, so that three soft knocks from `sound-effects/Door knock.m4a` sound there. A picture with more than one takes a list, as in `"effect": ["sword", "gallop"]`. Say which titles, dates or attributions are guesses. If the artist is new, add them to `puzzles/artists.json` too.

   Give the entry a `content` list if the picture shows any of these, and leave it out otherwise: `nudity` (any visible nudity at all, including the naked infant in a Madonna and Child, cherubs and nude statues), `violence` (fighting, weapons in use, wounds, blood, killed animals, martyrdoms, abductions, menace against women, as in Susanna and the Elders, and any reference to rape, in the title or the story the picture tells, however calm the picture looks, as in Lucretia or The Feast of the Gods) and `frightening` (monsters, menacing beasts, corpses, human skulls, people in peril). Settings can hide each kind, and Kids mode hides all three, so when unsure, tag it. Say which pictures were tagged.

4. **Check** — load the gallery in the preview and confirm the artist's card is there, every card on their page shows its picture and the new puzzle opens. Tap the splash away first. Kids mode must be off for this (Settings, hold Off for three seconds), or tagged pictures will be missing.

Thumbnails are part of adding a puzzle, never a separate request. If any listed puzzle is missing its file in `puzzles/thumbs/`, generate it.

## Pulling pictures from the National Gallery of Art

`node nga.js "Claude Monet"` fetches every open-access picture by that artist from the gallery's open data into `puzzles/images/` (4096 px on the longest side, as JPEGs named ready to use as the slug) and writes `puzzles/images/<surname>.nga.json` with the gallery's title, date, medium, credit line and a description of each picture. It skips pictures already downloaded. A second word keeps to one kind of work, for an artist the gallery also holds hundreds of prints by: `node nga.js "Mary Cassatt" Painting`. Then carry out the four steps above for each one, taking titles and dates from that file rather than guessing.

## Ancient art from the Met

The Ancient Art category has no artists: its cards are cultures, Ancient Egypt, Ancient Near East, Ancient Greece and Ancient Rome, each standing as the `artist` of its puzzles, and the slugs start `egypt-`, `near-east-`, `greece-` and `rome-` in place of a surname. Every one is from the Met's Open Access (CC0), recorded in `puzzles/images/<culture>.met.json`; the Met's CC0 covers its photographs, so a statue, vase or room may go in. Seven of the Egyptian puzzles are not ancient paintings but the copies of them that the Met's Egyptian Expedition painted in the tombs in 1908–1930s (Nina and Norman de Garis Davies, Charles K. Wilkinson), and their italic line says so; Nina Davies died in 1965 and Wilkinson in 1986, so they pass the 70-year test only because the Met, which made them, releases them as CC0. Its departments are Egyptian Art (10), Ancient West Asian Art (3) and Greek and Roman Art (13). The Met's API retired `/public/collection/v1/search` on 1 October 2026; search with `/public/collection/v1.1/search` (`q`, `departmentId`, `isHighlight`, `hasImages`, `limit`, `offset`), and ask slowly, a second or so apart, or its firewall answers with a page of HTML.

Anglo-Saxon and Saxon Art is made the same way, from the Met's Medieval Art and Cloisters departments: two cards, Anglo-Saxon England (slugs `anglo-saxon-`, the Met's culture "Anglo-Saxon", c. 450–1066) and Saxony (slugs `saxon-`, works the Met places in Saxony or Lower Saxony, or Ottonian, the Saxon emperors' art, to 1270), recorded in `puzzles/images/anglo-saxon.met.json` and `saxon.met.json`. The Met holds few Anglo-Saxon works with pictures, about twenty, so that card has nearly all of them.

**Japanese Prints is taken out of the app for now** and may come back. Everything it had is kept in `shelved/japanese-prints/`, which is git-ignored: the puzzle copies, thumbnails and blurs (`puzzles/`, `thumbs/`, `veils/`), copies of the Met records (`records/`), and in `entries.json` its period (which sat in Romantics & Realism, after Victorian Classicism), its ten artists and its 52 puzzle entries, ready to be put back into `periods.json`, `artists.json` and `puzzles.json`. The originals are in the `puzzle - monetize` folder. It was made like this. Japanese Prints is ukiyo-e from the Met's Asian Art department (CC0): ten artists from Harunobu to Yoshitoshi, five or six woodblock prints each, recorded in `puzzles/images/<surname>.met.json` and slugged by the art name the prints are known by (`hokusai-`, `hiroshige-`), which is also the last word of the `name`. The Met holds many impressions of the same print; take its own photograph of the one with the best colour, and leave out a photograph that shows a colour bar or a ruler, or crop it to the sheet and say so in the record, as was done for Yoshitoshi's Teasing the Cat. Leave out the Met's early 20th-century recuts (marked so in their dates), pillar prints too narrow to make a puzzle, and shunga.

## Pulling pictures from Wikimedia Commons

For an artist whose paintings hang where there is no open-access programme, as Caravaggio's do in Italian churches and museums. Wikidata lists an artist's paintings with the Commons file of each (`wdt:P170` for the artist, `wdt:P18` for the picture, `wdt:P373` for the Commons category, which often holds a larger file); the Commons API (`prop=imageinfo&iiprop=size|extmetadata|url`) gives each file's size and, in `LicenseShortName`, its licence. Keep only files marked "Public domain" or CC0 whose longest side is at least 2048 px. Download the original, or the 3840 px rendering (`iiurlwidth=3840`) of one larger than 5000 px, into `puzzles/images/` under the slug, sending a User-Agent that names the app. Write `puzzles/images/<surname>.commons.json` with each picture's `file`, `title`, `date`, `collection`, `wikidata` id, `source` (the Commons page), `licence`, `size` and `italianHeritageNotice` (whether the page carries the Italian heritage template).

Then look at every picture before carrying out the four steps: leave out black-and-white photographs of lost paintings, files that show a frame or a room, and pictures doubtfully attributed. Commons gives no dependable titles or dates, so take the usual scholarly ones and say that they are such. Where two pictures share a title, start the italic line with the city, as in "Paris, c. 1595–1598".

## Publishing

- Push to GitHub only when the user asks. The repo is public, so everything pushed is public. The pages at GitHub are a free preview of what will be sold, so the rights section applies to them in full.
- `puzzles/images/` (originals), `dist/`, `mockups/`, `shelved/` (work taken out of the app for now) and `.claude/` are git-ignored on purpose.
- After a push, GitHub Pages takes a minute or two to update.
- A home-screen app on the iPad is only paused when put away, and comes back running the copy it had. So each time it comes back it asks whether `index.html` has a new ETag (every publish stamps every file anew), and if so reloads, but only from the gallery's pages, landing on the home page (`checkFresh`, `reloadIfStale` in `app.js`). The local server sends no ETag, so it never reloads there. A device still on a copy from before this check needs the app closed and opened again once.

## Running locally

`node serve.js` serves the app on port 8000 (the preview uses `.claude/launch.json`).
